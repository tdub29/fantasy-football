# Invent Options for Mutual Gain

**Produce:** a broadened options list (10+ candidates from a no-commit brainstorm), with the differing interests dovetailed into "low-cost-to-me / high-benefit-to-them" trades and 2–3 "yesable propositions."

## When to use

Trigger when the deal looks zero-sum or one-dimensional. Concrete cues:

- "There's only one pie and we're fighting over slices."
- "It's a win/lose — either I get X or they do."
- Only creative move on the table is splitting the difference.
- The user has mapped interests and now needs solutions.
- A single number/variable is dominating ("just the base," "just the price").

## The framework

### Core thesis

Skilled negotiators **expand the pie before dividing it.** The children split the orange in half — then one eats the fruit and throws the peel, the other bakes with the peel and throws the fruit. Both could have had the whole of what they wanted. Inventing options is "one of the most useful assets a negotiator can have," yet negotiators rarely do it.

### The four obstacles to inventing

1. **Premature judgment** — a critical sense pounces on every idea; judgment hinders imagination. Worse under negotiation pressure and in the other side's presence (a half-baked idea may be taken as a commitment or leak your position).
2. **Searching for the single answer** — treating inventing as outside the job; premature closure.
3. **The assumption of a fixed pie** — either/or, "$100 more for you is $100 less for me."
4. **Thinking "solving their problem is their problem"** — refusing to make their decision attractive, which means no agreement at all.

### The four prescriptions

**1. Separate inventing from deciding.** Invent first, decide later. Run a **brainstorm** with the no-criticism rule:
- *Before*: define purpose, pick 5–8 people, change the environment, set an informal tone, choose a facilitator.
- *During*: seat people **side by side facing the problem**; outlaw negative criticism; generate a long list; record ideas in full view.
- *After*: star the promising ones ("What I like best is… might it be even better if…?"), invent improvements, then set a separate time to decide.
- Brainstorming **with** the other side is harder but powerful; flag it explicitly as not-a-negotiation, advance ideas in pairs/with options you obviously reject so nothing reads as an offer.

**2. Broaden your options** (don't hunt the one needle):
- **Circle Chart** — shuttle specific↔general: Problem → Diagnosis → General approaches → Action ideas. One good option reveals the theory behind it, which generates more options.
- **Different experts' eyes** — view the problem as a banker, lawyer, coach, economist would.
- **Agreements of different strength** — substantive↔procedural, permanent↔provisional, comprehensive↔partial, binding↔nonbinding, first-order↔second-order ("agree on where we disagree").
- **Change the scope** — fractionate ("edit chapter 1 for $300, see how it goes") or enlarge to sweeten the pot (the World Bank turning the Indus dispute into new irrigation projects).

**3. Look for mutual gain.**
- **Identify shared interests** — latent in every negotiation; they're *opportunities, not godsends* (make them explicit and future-oriented: a joint goal of "five new industries in three years"). An outcome where the other side gets nothing is worse for you — they won't keep to it.
- **Dovetail differing interests** — the engine of creative deals. Agreement is often based on *disagreement* (buyer thinks the stock rises, seller thinks it falls). Look for differences in **interests, beliefs, time-value, forecasts, and risk-aversion** (Law of the Sea: low fees while miners' risk is high, higher fees later — risk traded for revenue). Motto: **"Look for items of low cost to you and high benefit to them, and vice versa."**

**4. Make their decision easy.**
- Pick **whose shoes** (one real person, not "the company").
- Give them an **answer, not a problem** — reduce uncertainty; don't say "come up with something and I'll tell you if it's enough."
- Distinguish **words vs. performance** — "if you want a horse to jump a fence, don't raise the fence."
- Shape it as **legitimate** and find **precedent**; offers beat threats.
- Write a **"yesable proposition"** — a proposal where their single word "yes" is sufficient, realistic, and operational. **Ask for their preferences** between equally-acceptable variants to ratchet toward joint gains.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Name the obstacle** that's shrinking options (which of the four?).
2. **Run a no-commit brainstorm** — get 10+ markedly different ideas; postpone all judgment.
3. **Broaden** with Circle Chart, different-expert eyes, varied strength/scope.
4. **List shared interests** and reframe each as a concrete shared goal.
5. **Dovetail differences** — build a "low-cost-to-me / high-benefit-to-them" trade table across interests/beliefs/time/forecast/risk.
6. **Draft 2–3 yesable propositions** and pick variants to ask their preference between.
7. **Sanity-check**: did you decide too early, or genuinely expand before dividing?

## Apply to Trevor's offer close

Comp is multi-variable — that's the pie to expand. Brainstorm beyond base: sign-on bonus, accelerated equity vesting, level/title, scope (team, charter, what you own), refresh schedule, start date, remote terms, learning budget, an early review with a raise trigger. **Dovetail the differences**: they may guard *this fiscal year's cash* (forecast/time difference) while you value *level and equity upside* — so trade a flat base for a higher sign-on + a faster equity refresh + the Sr. level. Use forecast differences (a comp-revisit at 6 months tied to objective hit-the-ground metrics) the way the aging-football-star deal used base-plus-playoff-bonus. Then write the **yesable proposition** so the hiring manager can say "yes" without re-opening their banding. Hand precise market figures to `salary-negotiation-prep`.

## Common failure modes

- **Judging while inventing.** The no-criticism rule exists because judgment kills ideas.
- **Hunting the single best answer** instead of building room to negotiate.
- **Assuming a fixed pie** when differences could be dovetailed.
- **Leaving their decision hard.** No attractive option for them = no agreement.
- **Treating shared interests as automatic** rather than opportunities to make concrete.
- **One-variable tunnel vision** (just the base/price) when bundling unlocks the deal.
