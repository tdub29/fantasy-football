# Focus on Interests, Not Positions

**Produce:** an interests map — a two-column (you / them) table of interests behind each stated position, tagged shared / different / opposed, with basic-human-needs flagged.

## When to use

Trigger when the conversation is stuck on *what each side says it wants* rather than *why*. Concrete cues:

- "They want X, I want Y, and there's no overlap."
- "How do I get them to move off their number?"
- The user can state both sides' positions but not their underlying needs.
- A demand seems irrational until you ask what it's protecting.
- Prep for any deal: you need to map interests before inventing options.

## The framework

### Core thesis

The librarian opens the window in the *next* room — fresh air without a draft — because she asked *why*, not *how much*. "Your position is something you have decided upon; your interests are what caused you to so decide." Reconciling interests beats compromising positions for two reasons: (1) for every interest there are usually **several** positions that satisfy it (the obvious one is rarely the only one — see demilitarized Sinai), and (2) **behind opposed positions lie far more shared and compatible interests than conflicting ones.**

### Behind opposed positions: shared, different, and opposed interests

Don't assume opposed positions mean opposed interests. The tenant and landlord *both* want stability, a maintained apartment, and a good relationship; they have **differing** interests (paint allergy vs. repaint cost; timing of deposit) that don't conflict; only the rent figure is truly **opposed** — and once everything else is settled, the market often defines it. Agreement is often made possible **precisely because interests differ** (you'd rather have the shoes; the seller would rather have the money).

### How to identify interests

- **Ask "Why?"** — put yourself in their shoes and ask what need each position serves. Ask *them*, framed as understanding not justification: "What's your basic concern in wanting the lease capped at three years?"
- **Ask "Why not?"** — identify the decision you're asking them to make, then ask why they *haven't* made it. Build their **currently perceived choice** (the Iranian-student-leader balance sheet): list the pluses/minuses *they* see in saying yes vs. no. If you don't know what they think you're asking, they may not either.
- **Realize each side has multiple interests** — and the people on the other side don't all share the same ones (don't lump them into one "he," as LBJ did with North Vietnam). Every negotiator has a constituency.

### The most powerful interests are basic human needs

Money is often the surface; underneath sit **security, economic well-being, a sense of belonging, recognition, control over one's life.** Mexico burned its gas rather than be bullied on price — the interest was respect and equality, not just dollars. If a basic need feels threatened, no progress happens.

### Talking about interests

- **Make a list** so you can rank them and stimulate options.
- **Make your interests come alive** — be specific and concrete ("about 8:30 Tuesday a red gravel truck nearly hit seven-year-old Loretta"). Don't describe an ulcer as a mild stomachache.
- **Acknowledge their interests as part of the problem** before pressing yours ("As I understand it, your interests are X and Y — have I got that right?").
- **Put the problem before your answer** — reasoning first, conclusion last, or they'll stop listening.
- **Look forward, not back** — "Who should do what tomorrow?" not "justify yesterday."
- **Be concrete but flexible** — go in with *illustrative* options ("$5M would be the kind of figure that meets the interest…"), an open mind that is "not an empty one."
- **Be hard on the problem, soft on the people** — spend your aggressive energy advocating interests, while giving positive support to the human beings (cognitive dissonance makes this combination work).

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **List both sides' stated positions.**
2. For each, **ask Why / Why not** and write the interest(s) it serves.
3. **Build their currently perceived choice** if their position is puzzling — the yes/no balance sheet from their seat.
4. **Tag each interest** shared / different / opposed; **flag basic human needs.**
5. **Rank** your interests; note which are deal-breakers vs. tradeable.
6. **Draft framing language** — acknowledge-theirs-first, problem-before-answer, forward-looking.
7. **Sanity-check**: have you reduced everything to one opposed number, or surfaced the shared/differing interests that make a deal possible?

## Apply to Trevor's offer close

"$210k base" is a **position**. The interests beneath it: being paid at market (economic well-being + recognition), a level that matches scope and unlocks future trajectory (status, control), and runway/security. The employer's position ("budget is $185k") sits on interests too — internal equity/banding, headcount budget, precedent for the next hire, and *wanting you to actually accept and stay* (a shared interest). Map base / level / scope / equity / sign-on each to its interest, tag shared vs. opposed, and you'll find the differing interests — e.g., they care about this fiscal year's cash; you may care more about level and equity upside — that make a dovetailed package possible. Lead with "Here's what I'm trying to solve for…" before naming numbers.

## Common failure modes

- **Mistaking a position for an interest.** "$210k" / "Sr. title" are positions. Keep asking why.
- **Assuming symmetry of interests across their side.** The recruiter, hiring manager, and comp committee have different ones.
- **Reducing everything to the one opposed number** and missing the shared/differing interests.
- **Stating your conclusion first**, so they stop listening to the reasoning.
- **Arguing about the past** instead of "who does what tomorrow."
- **Going soft on the problem** to be nice — be hard on interests, soft on people.
