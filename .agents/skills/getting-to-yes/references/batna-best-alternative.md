# BATNA — Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement

**Produce:** a filled BATNA worksheet — your computed BATNA, your reservation point, your trip wire, an estimate of their BATNA, and a disclose/conceal decision.

## When to use

Trigger the moment leverage, acceptance, or "how hard can I push" is in question. Concrete cues:

- "What if they're more powerful / richer / bigger?"
- "Should I accept this offer?" or "How low should I be willing to go?"
- "How do I counter?" — almost always unanswerable until the BATNA exists.
- "Should I even negotiate this / is it worth it?"
- The user is anchoring on what they "ought" to get instead of what they'll actually do if there's no deal.

This is **the single most important negotiation artifact.** Build it before interests, options, or criteria when power is the issue.

## The framework

### Core thesis

The reason you negotiate is to do better than your alternative. So the standard against which every proposed agreement must be measured is not some arbitrary "bottom line" but your **BATNA — the best you can actually do if you walk away.** Power in negotiation is not wealth, connections, or muscle; it is **how attractive your no-deal option is.** The small town that knew it could annex and tax a factory at the full residential rate had more power than one of the world's largest corporations, which had committed to staying and built no alternative.

### Why a "bottom line" is the wrong tool

A predetermined bottom line (the lowest you'll accept) protects you from a terrible deal but carries high costs:
- It is **rigid** — set in advance, deaf to anything you learn at the table.
- It **kills imagination** — you stop inventing multi-variable solutions ("$235k *plus* first refusal on resale, delayed closing, the barn for two years…").
- It is usually **set wrong** — the family at the breakfast table inflates it; nobody objects to a higher number even when it's unrealistic.

The BATNA replaces it: a true measure that's also flexible.

### Reservation point vs. trip wire

- **Reservation point**: the value of your BATNA expressed in the deal's terms — the point of indifference between this deal and walking. Reject anything worse.
- **Trip wire**: a deliberately *better-than-BATNA* package you set as an early-warning line. When an offer dips below the trip wire, you pause and reexamine before agreeing — and it keeps margin in reserve if you later bring in a mediator. Like a bottom line, it can limit an agent's authority ("don't sell below $258k until you've talked to me").

### Developing (improving) your BATNA — three operations

The book is explicit: attractive alternatives rarely sit waiting; you *develop* them.
1. **Invent**: list every action you might take if no deal ("take the offer from Company Y," "stay put," "go back to market," "start consulting").
2. **Improve**: turn the most promising into *real* alternatives — get the Chicago job offer in hand, don't just imagine it.
3. **Select**: pick the one alternative you'd actually pursue. *That* is your BATNA.

The better your BATNA, the higher your minimum rises and the more forcefully you can advocate — "the more easily and happily you can walk away, the greater your capacity to affect the outcome."

### Two BATNA traps

- **Aggregate illusion**: you can't have the *sum* of all your alternatives ("California *or* grad school *or* a startup *or* Paris"). If the deal fails you get exactly **one**. Pick it; judge against it.
- **Over-optimism**: assuming many buyers/jobs/plumbers exist, and underrating the agony of the no-deal path (a lawsuit, a contested divorce, a strike).

### Estimate their BATNA too

The more you know of their alternatives, the better you can predict what to expect — and spot when **they** are over-optimistic (help them think it through). If their BATNA is genuinely strong, consider **changing it** (e.g., a community group files for permit revocation so the power company can no longer ignore them). And if *both* sides have great BATNAs, the best outcome may be **no deal, amicably reached** — a success, not a failure.

### Disclose or conceal?

- BATNA **better** than they think → **disclose** it ("there's another customer in the next room").
- BATNA **worse** than they think → **conceal**; disclosing weakens you.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **State the deal and the no-deal question.** "If we don't agree by [when], what will I actually do?"
2. **Invent** a list of no-deal actions.
3. **Improve** the top 2–3 into concrete, executable alternatives (get them as real as possible).
4. **Select** the single best — that's the BATNA. Name it in one sentence.
5. **Translate it into a reservation point** in the deal's currency (the offer value at which you're indifferent to walking).
6. **Set a trip wire** above the reservation point — the "pause and reexamine" line, with margin in reserve.
7. **Estimate their BATNA**; note whether they over- or under-rate it, and whether you can worsen it.
8. **Decide disclose vs. conceal.**
9. **Sanity-check**: did you fall for the aggregate illusion or over-optimism? Is the BATNA *executed*, not just imagined?

## Apply to Trevor's offer close

The book's own example is a job interview: walking in with *no* offers vs. *two competing offers* — "the difference is power." For the comp/level/scope/equity close:

- **BATNA = your strongest real alternative**: a competing offer, your current role, a counter from your employer, or a credible market re-entry. *Develop* it — get a competing offer in hand before the final call; don't negotiate on imagined leads.
- **Reservation point**: the total-comp / level / scope package below which walking beats signing. Compute it across the whole bundle (base + equity + level + scope + sign-on), not base alone — that's exactly the multi-variable flexibility a bottom line destroys.
- **Trip wire**: e.g., "if the level is below Sr. *and* base is under $X, pause and re-open the full package."
- **Their BATNA**: how badly do they need *you* vs. the next candidate? If they over-rate their pipeline, you may help them see your specific value.
- **Disclose** a strong competing offer; **conceal** a weak alternative.
- Hand the *numbers/market data* to `salary-negotiation-prep`; this doc owns the **walk-away logic and leverage**.

## Common failure modes

- **No BATNA, so no leverage.** Negotiating without one is "negotiating with your eyes closed." Build it first.
- **Bottom line masquerading as BATNA.** An arbitrary floor is rigid and usually wrong; the BATNA is derived from a real alternative.
- **Imagined, not developed.** "I could always go elsewhere" is not a BATNA until "elsewhere" is a concrete option you've made real.
- **Aggregate illusion.** Treating the menu of alternatives as your fallback; you only get one.
- **Ignoring their BATNA.** You can't estimate what's achievable, spot their over-optimism, or know whether to walk.
- **Mis-deciding disclosure.** Revealing a weak BATNA, or hiding a strong one.
