# Kahneman — Regression to the Mean

## The framework

> "Regression to the Mean." (Ch 17, lines 6382–6766)

Extreme outcomes are partly skill (or its absence) and partly luck. The luck component is, by definition, not repeatable. So extreme outcomes tend to be followed by less extreme ones — regardless of any intervention.

System 1 doesn't see regression. It sees patterns and reaches for causal explanations.

### The famous flight-school example

Israeli flight instructors believed PRAISE made trainees worse and PUNISHMENT made them better. Their evidence: after an exceptionally good landing, the next landing was usually worse; after an exceptionally bad landing, the next was usually better.

The actual explanation: regression to the mean. Both "exceptionally good" and "exceptionally bad" landings had luck components. The next landing reverted toward the trainee's actual skill level. The praise and the punishment had nothing to do with it.

### Three named sub-rules

**1. Regression appears wherever performance has a luck component**
> Test scores, sales numbers, athletic performance, business results, medical readings — anywhere skill + noise produce the observation, extreme observations are partly noise.

**2. Regression looks causal**
> The mind generates a causal story for the reversion. "The bestseller fell off because the marketing budget was cut" — or it just regressed. "The new CEO turned the company around" — or the previous quarter was an extreme low.

**3. Intervention timing creates illusory effectiveness**
> Interventions applied at extreme times (when things were exceptionally bad) get credit for the normal regression toward average. Coaching changes after a bad season. Medical treatments started during a flare-up. Both can look more effective than they are.

### The correlation rule

Regression is fully determined by correlation. If the correlation between observation 1 and observation 2 is 0.30, then the prediction for observation 2 should be 70% of the way from the mean toward observation 1 — NOT at the same extreme.

## How to apply

When an extreme outcome occurs:

1. **Predict regression for the NEXT observation.** Whatever extreme just happened, expect the next to be closer to average.
2. **Don't attribute the regression to a cause.** If you can't explain WHY things got back to normal without invoking "we did X," you're probably overestimating X.
3. **For interventions applied at extremes: discount the effect.** Some of the "improvement" is just regression.
4. **For honest evaluation: compare against a control group OR a matched non-extreme baseline.** Otherwise you can't separate intervention from regression.
5. **For your own performance: don't extrapolate from a single great or terrible result.** Average over a window.

## Common failure modes

- **"It worked!" after an intervention applied during a crisis.** Most crises resolve partly through regression. Without a control, you can't tell.
- **Punishing performance that was just unlucky.** The next observation will be better anyway; the punisher takes credit.
- **Hot streaks treated as the new normal.** They're not. Regression brings you back.
- **The "Sports Illustrated cover curse."** Players make the cover after extreme performance. Their next season regresses. The cover gets blamed.
- **Mistaking correlation strength for predictability.** If correlation is 0.30, your "confident" prediction should still be near the mean. Most people predict at the same extreme.
