# Kahneman — Availability Heuristic

## The framework

> "Availability heuristic: judging the frequency of a class by the ease with which instances come to mind." (Ch 12)

When asked "how common is X?", System 1 substitutes "how easily can I think of examples of X?" — and answers THAT instead. The substituted question is easier; it's also often misleading.

### What boosts availability (and thus distorts frequency estimates upward)

1. **Recency** — recent examples feel more frequent.
2. **Vividness** — dramatic examples feel more frequent.
3. **Personal experience** — events you've personally witnessed feel more frequent.
4. **Media coverage** — heavily-reported events feel more frequent (terror attacks).
5. **Emotional content** — events with strong emotional valence feel more frequent (deaths from sharks vs. deer).

### The classic findings

- People estimate that deaths from accidents are about as common as deaths from disease. (Disease kills ~18x more.)
- People rate stroke as less likely than all accidents combined. (Stroke kills ~2x more.)
- After a recent local fire, people increase their estimates of fire risk for weeks.

### Availability and emotion

> "Emotional, vivid, easy to imagine, and specific events tend to be available." (Ch 13)

This is why media coverage of plane crashes makes flying feel dangerous (per-mile, it's the safest mode of transit). And why a single anecdote about a friend defrauded online crowds out base-rate statistics.

## How to apply

When estimating frequency, risk, or probability:

1. **Ask: am I estimating frequency or just recalling vivid examples?** They're different.
2. **Look up base rates.** Actual statistics. For anything mortality- or risk-related, the gap between availability and reality is often 5–50x.
3. **For decisions about rare events: distinguish "easy to imagine" from "likely to happen."** Vivid scenarios feel inevitable; usually aren't.
4. **For organizational planning: the loudest recent crisis dominates planning.** Force discussion of risks NOT recently experienced.
5. **For yourself: emotional events distort proportionate response.** A friend's fraud doesn't change the fraud rate of the population; it just makes it more available.

## Common failure modes

- **Reading "but I've seen so many examples!" as evidence of frequency.** You've seen vivid examples; the boring counter-examples didn't make news.
- **Calibrating risk to media coverage.** Coverage tracks newsworthiness, not frequency.
- **Personal experience as base rate.** Your sample is too small and too biased to estimate population frequencies.
- **Availability cascades.** Initial event → media coverage → public concern → more media → more concern. The actual frequency doesn't change; the perceived frequency snowballs.
