# Kahneman — Peak-End Rule

## The framework

> "Peak-end rule: The global retrospective rating was well predicted by the average of the level of pain reported at the worst moment of the experience and at its end." (Ch 35, lines 13860–13862)

How the Remembering Self stores an experience:
- The PEAK (most intense moment, good or bad)
- The END (how it concluded)
- (Almost ignore: duration, average across moments, anything between peak and end)

### Three named sub-rules

**1. Duration neglect**
> "The duration of the procedure had no effect whatsoever on the ratings of total pain." (lines 13863–13864)

A 60-minute moderately painful procedure can be remembered as no worse than a 30-minute moderately painful procedure, if peak and end are similar.

**2. Lowering peak intensity can matter MORE than shortening total duration**
> "Lowering the peak intensity of pain could be more important than minimizing the duration." (lines 13889–13890)

If you can't avoid pain entirely, blunt the peak. The remembered experience will be better than a shorter, more intense version.

**3. Gradual relief beats abrupt end**
> "Gradual relief may be preferable to abrupt relief if patients retain a better memory when the pain at the end of the procedure is relatively mild." (lines 13891–13893)

Famous Redelmeier/Kahneman colonoscopy study: adding a few minutes of MILD discomfort at the end of a procedure produced better remembered experience than ending abruptly at high discomfort — even though the longer procedure had more total pain.

### The Two Selves

> The Experiencing Self vs. the Remembering Self. (Ch 35)

The Experiencing Self has the moments. The Remembering Self has the story. They have different preferences, and the Remembering Self usually wins because that's who decides whether to repeat.

So:
- A vacation evaluated moment-by-moment: best with longer duration of pleasant moments.
- A vacation evaluated retrospectively: depends on peak and end.

These can diverge sharply. Most decisions are made by the Remembering Self.

## How to apply

When designing an experience:

1. **Identify the peak.** What's the most intense moment? Is it as good as possible? (Or for unavoidable pain: as low-peak as possible?)
2. **Design the end.** What's the LAST 10–20% of the experience? Is it landing well? Even if the rest was great, a bad ending lands as a bad experience.
3. **Don't over-invest in extending duration.** A longer pleasant experience isn't necessarily a more memorable one. Spend that effort on peak/end instead.
4. **For unavoidable pain: blunt the peak; finish on a milder note.** Don't end at the worst moment.

When evaluating a past experience:

1. **Ask: am I evaluating moments or memory?** Different answers.
2. **What was the peak? What was the end?** Likely those two are doing most of the work.
3. **If you keep refusing to repeat something you "loved": check whether the end was bad.** The Remembering Self might be over-weighting an ending.

## Common failure modes

- **Optimizing for moment-by-moment satisfaction in a context where memory is what drives future decisions.** Trip to Disneyland: optimize the day for the family's moments OR for the memory? Different designs.
- **Letting an experience end at its worst moment.** A concert that ends after the last hard song. A meeting that ends in an argument. A vacation that ends with the lost-luggage day. All these distort the remembered experience disproportionately.
- **Believing more time = more memory weight.** Duration is what gets neglected. Adding hours doesn't proportionately add to memory.
- **Designing for the Experiencing Self when the decision will be made by the Remembering Self.** Mismatch produces a paradox: people refuse to repeat experiences whose moments were great.
