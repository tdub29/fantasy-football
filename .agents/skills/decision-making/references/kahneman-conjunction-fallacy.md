# Kahneman — The Conjunction Fallacy (Linda Problem)

## The framework

> "Linda: Less Is More." (Ch 15)

A logical rule: the probability of A AND B is always less than or equal to the probability of A alone. Adding a condition NARROWS the set of cases that fit. So a more detailed scenario is always less likely than its less detailed parent.

System 1 violates this constantly because it judges by representativeness/coherence — and a vivid specific scenario can FEEL more representative than a vague general one.

### The Linda problem

"Linda is 31, single, outspoken, very bright. She majored in philosophy. As a student, she was deeply concerned with discrimination and social justice, and also participated in antinuclear demonstrations."

Rank by probability:
- (a) Linda is a bank teller.
- (b) Linda is a bank teller AND is active in the feminist movement.

89% of subjects ranked (b) > (a). But (b) is a SUBSET of (a). It cannot be more probable.

Why people make the error: (b) FITS the description better. Representativeness is high; probability rules are ignored.

### Three named sub-cases

**1. Detailed scenarios feel more probable**
> A detailed business plan ("Apple will release a new phone with feature X, priced at Y, in Q3 2026") feels more probable than the general claim ("Apple will release a new phone in 2026") — because it's more concrete. Logically, it's strictly less likely.

**2. Adding sympathetic details increases willingness-to-pay**
> Subjects offered insurance against "death from any cause on your trip" vs. "death from a terrorist attack on your trip" — paid MORE for the specific narrower one, despite it covering a strict subset.

**3. Joint forecasts**
> A forecaster who predicts 5 specific things is rated more impressive than one who predicts 2 specific things — even though predicting 5 makes it MUCH less likely all are right.

## How to apply

When evaluating a probability claim:

1. **Identify the conjunction.** Is the claim "A and B and C and D"? Each AND reduces probability.
2. **Compute (mentally) P(each component).** Even rough estimates show how fast conjunctions decay. P(A=0.5) × P(B|A=0.5) × P(C=0.5) = 12.5%, not 50%.
3. **Prefer broader to narrower predictions.** "Apple will release a new phone" beats "Apple will release a new phone with feature X at price Y in Q3."
4. **For scenario planning: don't fall in love with the detailed one.** The 30-page detailed scenario feels more real than the 1-paragraph general one. The detailed one is strictly less likely to occur as specified.
5. **In risk assessment: beware narrow specific risks crowding out broad diffuse ones.** "Plane crash on this specific airline on this specific route" feels more vivid than "any travel-related fatality this year" — but the broader category is what should drive insurance/planning.

## Common failure modes

- **Confusing plausibility with probability.** A vivid story is plausible. Plausibility ≠ probability.
- **Confusing specificity with predictive value.** Specific predictions seem to "say more." They also have to overcome a higher logical bar.
- **Mistaking better-fitting for more-likely.** Better fit with the stereotype/description is exactly what representativeness rewards. It is NOT what probability rewards.
