# Tetlock — The Ten Commandments for Aspiring Superforecasters

## The framework

> "Guidelines are the best we can do in a world where nothing is certain or exactly repeatable." (Appendix: Ten Commandments for Aspiring Superforecasters)

The appendix distills the whole book into a usable operating procedure — ten guidelines (plus an eleventh that tells you not to treat them as rigid). Each is itself a balancing act between opposing errors; mastering them is the "error-balancing bicycle" you can only learn by doing. Use this as the master checklist; the other references expand individual commandments in depth.

## The ten (as a checklist)

**1. Triage.**
Spend effort where it pays off. Skip "clocklike" questions a rule of thumb already answers, and "cloud-like" questions even the best models can't beat. Concentrate on the **Goldilocks zone** of difficulty. Ask: am I about to waste time on the unpredictable, or fail to try on the predictable?

**2. Break seemingly intractable problems into tractable sub-problems.**
Channel Enrico Fermi: decompose the question into knowable and unknowable parts, flush out hidden assumptions, and make explicit best guesses for each piece. Better to expose errors quickly than hide them behind vague verbiage. (See `references/tetlock-fermi-decomposition.md`.)

**3. Strike the right balance between inside and outside views.**
Nothing is 100% unique. Find a comparison class and ask "how often do things of this sort happen in situations of this sort?" Anchor on that base rate (outside view), then adjust for the specifics of this case (inside view). (See `references/tetlock-outside-then-inside-view.md`.)

**4. Strike the right balance between under- and overreacting to evidence.**
Belief updating is like flossing — boring but it pays off. Update incrementally on subtle signals (0.6 → 0.65), but also know when to jump fast on diagnostic news. Avoid both inertia and whipsaw. (See `references/tetlock-belief-updating.md`.)

**5. Look for the clashing causal forces at work in each problem.**
For every good argument there's usually a worthwhile counterargument. List, in advance, the signs that would push you toward the other side. Synthesize opposing views into one image — the dragonfly eye — rather than picking a side and defending it.

**6. Strive to distinguish as many degrees of doubt as the problem permits — but no more.**
Your uncertainty dial needs more than three settings. "Maybe" is nearly useless. Separate 60/40 from 55/45. Don't reserve rigorous probability for trivial pursuits like sports brackets while shrugging "fifty-fifty" on serious questions. (See `references/tetlock-calibration-brier-granularity.md`.)

**7. Strike the right balance between under- and overconfidence, between prudence and decisiveness.**
Manage the trade-off between being a waffler (no one listens) and a blowhard (no one should). Long-term accuracy needs both good **calibration** and good **resolution** — tamp down both misses and false alarms.

**8. Look for the errors behind your mistakes, but beware of rearview-mirror hindsight biases.**
Own your failures; run unflinching postmortems on where exactly you went wrong. But also run postmortems on **successes** — you may have just lucked out via offsetting errors. Don't over-learn or under-learn. (See `references/tetlock-keep-score-postmortem.md`.)

**9. Bring out the best in others and let others bring out the best in you.**
Master team craft: **perspective taking** (restate the other side's argument to their satisfaction), **precision questioning** (help others sharpen their claims), and **constructive confrontation** (disagree without being disagreeable). "Managing is like holding a dove in your hand" — too tight kills it, too loose loses it.

**10. Master the error-balancing bicycle.**
Every commandment requires balancing opposing errors, and you can't learn balance from a textbook. Superforecasting is the product of deep, deliberate practice with unambiguous feedback — not casually tossing out probabilities while skimming the news.

**11. (The anti-commandment) Don't treat commandments as commandments.**
"It is impossible to lay down binding rules, because two cases will never be exactly the same." These are guidelines requiring constant mindfulness, especially when you're dutifully trying to follow them.

## How to apply

1. Run the list top-to-bottom as a forecasting checklist: **triage → decompose → outside-then-inside → assign a granular probability → identify clashing forces → set under/overconfidence → commit a number → schedule the postmortem.**
2. For any commandment that's the crux of the current question, open its dedicated reference and apply that framework in full.
3. Treat each one as a tension to balance, not a rule to maximize — the skill is finding the middle, and that comes from practice plus feedback.
4. End every nontrivial forecast at #8/#10: log it so you can later score it and learn.

## Common failure modes

- **Skipping triage** — pouring effort into a cloud-like question that no method can crack.
- **Treating the commandments as a rigid algorithm** — violating #11; the value is in balanced judgment, not box-checking.
- **Stopping at the inside view** — ignoring #3's base-rate anchor.
- **Three-setting uncertainty** — violating #6 by answering "maybe."
- **No feedback loop** — forecasting without #8/#10 scorekeeping, so no practice-driven improvement is possible.
