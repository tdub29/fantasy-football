# Tetlock — Belief Updating

## The framework

> "Belief updating is to good forecasting as brushing and flossing are to good dental hygiene. It can be boring, occasionally uncomfortable, but it pays off in the long term." (Chapter 10; Commandment 4)

Forecasts are not one-shot pronouncements. As news arrives, a superforecaster revises the number — frequently, and usually in **small increments**. The skill is to update in the **Bayesian spirit** (move your probability toward the evidence, proportional to how diagnostic it is) without needing to do the formal math, while avoiding two opposite errors.

### The two errors to balance

- **Underreaction (anchoring / inertia).** Clinging to your prior, treating new evidence as noise, refusing to move because changing your mind feels like admitting you were wrong. Often compounded by commitment and reputational fear — the public tends to read belief-updating as confusion rather than rationality, so people dig in.
- **Overreaction (whipsaw).** Lurching on every fresh headline, getting "suckered by misleading clues," chasing volatility, and abandoning a sound prior because of one dramatic but non-diagnostic event.

The best forecasters are **incremental updaters**: moving from 0.4 to 0.35, or 0.6 to 0.65 — distinctions too subtle for vague verbiage but exactly where good-versus-great is decided. Crucially, they **also know how to jump** — to move fast and far when a signal is genuinely diagnostic. The art is telling a subtle real clue from a misleading one.

### Bayesian in spirit, not in formula

Lab studies give clean Bayesian answers (drawing balls from an urn, the correct update might be 0.92), and most people **underreact**, landing nearer 70%. Superforecasters are measurably better Bayesians than regular forecasters — not because they compute Bayes' theorem, but because they **value the skill and work at it**: they ask "how diagnostic is this evidence — how much more likely is it if my hypothesis is true than if it's false?" and nudge accordingly.

### Practical signal-finding

- **Hunt for non-obvious lead indicators** — "what would have to happen before X could?" — and watch those, getting to telltale clues before everyone else.
- **Diagnosticity test** — before moving, ask whether the new fact actually discriminates between outcomes, or just feels important. (A critical article in the official Chinese press: real signal China will squeeze Pyongyang, or editorial quirk?)
- **Resist wishful thinking** — the lure to update toward the outcome you *want*.
- **Beware over-updating on confirming news** too — a string of small confirmations can build false confidence.

## How to apply

1. **Re-forecast on a cadence and on news.** Treat each estimate as provisional (perpetual beta); revisit it when relevant evidence lands.
2. **Default to small increments.** Nudge the number a few points; reserve big jumps for genuinely diagnostic evidence.
3. **Run the diagnosticity check** before moving: is this evidence much more likely under one hypothesis than the other? Size the update to the answer.
4. **Watch for the two failure directions.** If you haven't moved in a while despite real news, suspect underreaction; if you're moving on every headline, suspect overreaction.
5. **Separate the signal from your wishes.** Note when the update you're tempted to make happens to favor the outcome you prefer.
6. **Log each move with its trigger** so the postmortem can later judge whether your updates were warranted (`references/tetlock-keep-score-postmortem.md`).

## Common failure modes

- **Anchoring on the prior** — refusing to budge because changing your mind feels like losing face.
- **Headline whipsaw** — overreacting to dramatic but non-diagnostic news.
- **Missing the jump** — incrementing timidly when a genuinely diagnostic signal warrants a large, fast move.
- **Wishful updating** — moving toward the outcome you want rather than the one the evidence supports.
- **Silent updates** — changing the number without recording what evidence drove it, making the move impossible to learn from.
