# Tetlock — The Superforecaster Mindset

## The framework

> "What makes them so good is less what they are than what they do — the hard work of research, the careful thought and self-criticism, the gathering and synthesizing of other perspectives, the granular judgments and relentless updating." (Chapters 5, 8)

Superforecasters are not geniuses with secret data. They are ordinary smart people with a particular **disposition** and a set of **habits**. *Superforecasting* argues that forecasting is a skill — measurable, teachable, and improvable with deliberate practice and honest feedback. The mindset is the soil; the techniques in the other references only grow in it.

### The three pillars of the disposition

**1. Actively open-minded thinking**
Treat your beliefs as hypotheses to be tested, not treasures to be guarded. Actively seek out evidence and arguments that could prove you wrong, and weight them fairly. The opposite — defending the "tip-of-your-nose" perspective that feels obviously correct — is the default human setting, and it is the enemy of accuracy. Superforecasters score high on tests of actively open-minded thinking; it is one of the strongest correlates of accuracy in the Good Judgment Project.

**2. Growth mindset (Carol Dweck)**
Belief that ability is built through effort, not fixed at birth. Forecasters who believe they can get better actually do, because they put in the deliberate practice and absorb the feedback. A fixed mindset ("I'm just not a numbers person") becomes self-fulfilling — it stops the practice that produces improvement. Tetlock's data show the role of raw intelligence *shrinks* over time relative to acquired skill: practice really does make forecasters better.

**3. Perpetual beta**
Tech term for software that is permanently being upgraded. The strongest single predictor of rising accuracy is "perpetual beta — the degree to which one is committed to belief updating and self-improvement." Superforecasters treat every forecast as a draft, every outcome as data, and themselves as a work in progress that is never finished.

### Foxes, not hedgehogs — and the dragonfly eye

Borrowing Isaiah Berlin's image (via Tetlock's earlier *Expert Political Judgment*):

- **Hedgehogs** know "one big thing." They view the world through a single grand theory (Marxism, free-market fundamentalism, a pet ideology), force every event to fit it, and forecast with confident, quotable certainty. They are great on TV and poor at prediction.
- **Foxes** know "many little things." They are skeptical of grand schemes, stitch together many sources and models, tolerate contradiction and doubt, and speak in qualifications. They forecast better.

The superforecaster is a fox with the **dragonfly eye**: a dragonfly's compound eye fuses thousands of slightly different perspectives into one superior image. Aggregating many independent viewpoints — your own multiple models, plus other people's — produces foresight that no single perspective can match. Aggregation does not come naturally; the tip-of-your-nose view insists it already sees reality correctly. The work is to consult other lenses anyway.

## How to apply

1. **Hold beliefs as hypotheses.** Before answering, ask: "What would change my mind? What evidence am I avoiding because it's inconvenient?" Go look for it.
2. **Adopt the growth-mindset frame explicitly.** Forecasting accuracy is trainable. If you're bad at it now, that's a starting point, not a verdict.
3. **Stay in perpetual beta.** Date and log each forecast as a draft. Plan to revise it. Never treat a judgment as final until the question resolves.
4. **Think like a fox.** Resist the one-big-theory pull. Deliberately gather several independent models or sources, including ones from people you disagree with.
5. **Build the dragonfly eye.** Aggregate perspectives: your inside-view model, the outside-view base rate, a devil's-advocate read, and others' forecasts. Synthesize rather than picking the loudest single view.
6. **Pair disposition with technique.** The mindset is necessary but not sufficient — route to `references/tetlock-ten-commandments.md` for the operating procedure that the disposition powers.

## Common failure modes

- **Hedgehog certainty.** Forcing every question through one grand theory and forecasting with telegenic overconfidence. Confident and quotable is not the same as accurate.
- **Fixed mindset.** "I'm not a math/probability person" — a belief that prevents the practice that would disprove it.
- **Treating forecasts as final.** Issuing a judgment and emotionally defending it instead of staying in perpetual beta.
- **Single-perspective lock-in.** Trusting the tip-of-your-nose view because it feels objectively correct, and never consulting other lenses.
- **Confusing intelligence with the disposition.** Smart people who lack active open-mindedness forecast no better than average; the disposition, not the IQ, carries most of the signal over time.
