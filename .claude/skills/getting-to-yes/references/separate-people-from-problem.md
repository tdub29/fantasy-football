# Separate the People from the Problem

**Produce:** a people-problem triage — each people issue sorted into perception / emotion / communication, with the specific move to make for each (and your own people problems listed too).

## When to use

Trigger when the relationship has become entangled with the substance. Concrete cues:

- "It got personal." / "They're attacking me, not the problem."
- Emotions (anger, defensiveness, fear) are stalling the deal.
- The two sides are misreading each other's intentions.
- The user is about to trade a substantive concession to repair a relationship (the trap).
- Prep: you want to build the relationship *before* the hard conversation.

This skill owns the **method** for people problems. For the in-the-moment verbal move ("what do I literally say when they snap at me"), defer to `difficult-conversation`.

## The framework

### Core thesis

"Negotiators are people first." You deal with human beings who have emotions, egos, and partisan perceptions — and so do you. The relationship tends to get **entangled** with the substance: "The kitchen is a mess" is heard as a personal attack. The fix is to **be soft on the people and hard on the problem** — deal with people issues *directly* using psychological techniques, *not* by buying them off with substantive concessions. The 3rd edition is emphatic: this means **disentangle**, not ignore — make people issues a priority start to finish.

The three baskets: every people problem is one of **perception, emotion, or communication.**

### Perception — conflict is in their heads, not the object

"Understanding the other side's thinking is not simply a useful activity… *their thinking is the problem.*" Moves:
- **Put yourself in their shoes** — understand the *force* with which they hold their view; "know what it feels like to be a beetle," not study it under glass. (The tenant/landlady perception list: every fact reads opposite from each chair.)
- **Don't deduce their intentions from your fears.**
- **Don't blame them for your problem** — distinguish symptom from person.
- **Discuss each other's perceptions** explicitly; communicate things they'd be glad to hear (the cheap, overlooked investment).
- **Act inconsistently with their perceptions** (Sadat flying to Jerusalem).
- **Give them a stake** — let them participate so they own the outcome ("the process is the product").
- **Face-saving** — make proposals consistent with their values, principles, and past words; this is not pretense, it's letting them reconcile the deal with their self-image.

### Emotion — feelings can matter more than talk

- **Recognize and understand emotions, theirs and yours** — write down what you feel and want to feel; do the same for them.
- **Ask what's producing the emotion**; attend to the five **core concerns**: autonomy, appreciation, affiliation, role, status. Watch for **identity threats** (self-image as competent/fair/lovable).
- **Make emotions explicit and acknowledge them as legitimate.**
- **Let the other side let off steam** — listen quietly, don't react; the "only one person gets angry at a time" rule.
- **Use symbolic gestures** — an apology, a note of sympathy, a shared meal: high impact, low cost.

### Communication — three problems, with fixes

Problems: (1) not really talking to each other (playing to the gallery); (2) not hearing (busy framing your reply); (3) misunderstanding (the "mediator/compromise" Persian mistranslation that got Waldheim's car stoned). Fixes:
- **Listen actively and acknowledge** — "the cheapest concession you can make is to let them know they've been heard"; put their case *better than they can*, then respond.
- **Speak to be understood** — you're two judges working a joint opinion, not persuading a jury; meet privately/small.
- **Speak about yourself, not them** — "I feel let down," not "you broke your word."
- **Speak for a purpose** — sometimes less disclosure is better; know what the statement is for.

### Prevention beats cure

Build a working relationship *before* the negotiation (Franklin's borrow-a-book trick). And **face the problem, not the people** — sit literally side by side with the contract/map/pad in front of you, like two shipwrecked sailors treating survival as a shared problem.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **List every people issue** currently in play — include **your own**, not just theirs.
2. **Sort each** into perception / emotion / communication.
3. For each, **select the specific move** from the framework (e.g., perception → "give them a stake"; emotion → "acknowledge as legitimate + let off steam"; communication → "active-listen + speak about yourself").
4. **Check for identity threats and core concerns** (autonomy/appreciation/affiliation/role/status).
5. **Draft "I"-statements** to replace any "you"-accusations.
6. **Plan a side-by-side framing** ("we're both X; let's face this together").
7. **Sanity-check**: are you about to fix a people problem with a *substantive* concession? Don't — handle it on the people track.

## Apply to Trevor's offer close

The comp talk runs through *people*: a recruiter who feels you're being "difficult," a hiring manager whose identity as a fair leader feels challenged by a counter, your own anxiety reading their silence as rejection (don't deduce intentions from fears). Moves that matter: **acknowledge their constraints as legitimate** ("I get that bands and internal equity are real for you"); use **"I" framing** ("I'm trying to land at a level that matches the scope," not "your offer is lowball"); **give them a stake** by co-drafting the package so the manager owns selling it upward (face-saving — they present the number as their win); and **frame side-by-side** ("we both want me to start excited and stay — let's solve the package together"). Keep this on the people track so you never trade away base just to seem agreeable.

## Common failure modes

- **Fixing people problems with substantive concessions** — the core error this chapter exists to prevent.
- **"Soft on both."** Soft on people must pair with *hard on the problem*, or it's the soft-bargaining trap.
- **Deducing intentions from fears**; putting the worst reading on their words.
- **Blaming** — entangles people with problem and triggers defense.
- **Ignoring your own people issues** — your anger/perception/listening are half the problem.
- **Treating face-saving as trivial pretense** rather than a real need.
- **Drifting into verbal tactics** that belong to `difficult-conversation`.
