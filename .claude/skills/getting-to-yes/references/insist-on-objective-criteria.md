# Insist on Using Objective Criteria

**Produce:** a criteria table — candidate fair standards (and/or fair procedures) for each contested issue, each tested for reciprocity, plus a "what's-your-theory" script for the contested number.

## When to use

Trigger when interests genuinely conflict and it's becoming a contest of wills. Concrete cues:

- "What's a *fair* number / how do I justify mine?"
- "They just keep insisting; it's willpower vs. willpower."
- "They said it's company policy / take it or leave it."
- The user wants to anchor on something defensible (market value, precedent).
- Two legitimate standards point to different numbers and you need to choose.

## The framework

### Core thesis

However well you map interests and invent options, you'll still hit conflicting interests "win-win" can't paper over. Resolving them by **will** is costly — inefficient, relationship-damaging, and unwise. The alternative: settle on a basis **independent of the will of either side** — **objective criteria.** "Commit yourself to reaching a solution based on principle, not pressure. Concentrate on the merits, not the mettle of the parties. Be open to reason, but closed to threats." The contractor who says "I gave you steel girders, now give me shallow foundations" gets answered with safety standards, not a horse-trade.

### Developing objective criteria

Prepare standards in advance. There's usually **more than one** available:
- **Fair standards**: market value, precedent, what a court would decide, professional/scientific/moral standards, efficiency, cost, equal treatment, reciprocity, tradition. (For a totaled car: depreciated cost, resale value, blue-book, replacement cost, what a court would award.)
- Criteria should be **independent of will**, **legitimate**, **practical**, and **reciprocally applicable** — the test: would they accept this standard if the roles were reversed? (Ask the realtor if their standard form is the one they use when *buying* a house.)

### Fair procedures (when a fair standard is elusive)

Produce a will-independent outcome via process:
- **"One cuts, the other chooses"** (and its variant — agree the arrangement *before* deciding roles; settle visitation before deciding custody).
- **Taking turns, drawing lots, letting someone else decide** (expert, mediator, arbitrator).
- **Last-best-offer arbitration** (baseball) — each side must make its final offer reasonable.
- Law of the Sea: a miner proposes two sites, the Enterprise picks one — harnessing the miner's expertise for mutual gain.

### Negotiating with objective criteria — three rules

1. **Frame each issue as a joint search for criteria.** "You want a high price, I want low — let's figure out what's *fair*. What standards apply?"
2. **Reason and be open to reason** about which standard fits. Behave like a judge: be willing to apply *their* standard or another, and when two legitimate standards conflict, look for a meta-basis (which has been used before, which is more widely applied) — or legitimately split the difference between two equally fair results.
3. **Never yield to pressure, only to principle.** Pressure = bribes, threats, manipulative trust appeals, refusal to budge. The response is always the same: invite their reasoning, propose the criterion you think applies, refuse to move except on that basis.

Key levers: **"Ask 'What's your theory?'"** when they state a number ("How did you arrive at that?"). **Each standard *they* propose becomes a lever** you turn back on them — their deferring to their own standard is an act of consistency, not weakness. If they won't budge and offer no principled basis, you have a take-it-or-leave-it choice measured against your BATNA.

### The Tom-vs-insurance-adjuster model

Adjuster: "$13,600. Company policy." Tom never counters with willpower; he asks **what standard** produced it, supplies replacement-cost evidence ("found a comparable car for $17,700"), and adjusts for mileage and packages line by line against the adjuster's *own* book — walking out with a check for **$18,024.** Principle as "your hardhearted partner who will not let you yield to pressure" — a form of "right makes might."

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **List the genuinely opposed issues** (from the interests map).
2. For each, **brainstorm 3–5 candidate fair standards.**
3. **Test each for reciprocity** — would it bind both sides?
4. Where no clean standard exists, **propose a fair procedure** instead.
5. **Draft the "What's your theory?" script** for the contested number.
6. **Plan the response to pressure** ("never yield to pressure, only to principle").
7. **Sanity-check**: are you using criteria as a *joint search*, or just as ammunition for a position you've pre-decided? (The latter isn't principled negotiation.)

## Apply to Trevor's offer close

This is the principle that maps **directly to comp benchmarking.** Don't argue "I deserve more" (will); anchor on standards: market data for the role/level/location (levels.fyi-type bands, salary surveys), internal equity / leveling rubrics, your competing offer as a market signal, scope-to-level fit. Open with the joint-search frame: "Let's anchor on what's fair for a Sr. Data Scientist with this scope — what market data are you using?" When they say **"company policy"** or **"that's the band,"** run the Tom move: *ask what standard set the band, then supply better evidence and adjust line by line* (level, scope, specialization premium). ## Common failure modes

- **Willpower dressed as principle** — "it's a matter of principle" as a battle cry. Principled means open to reasoned persuasion.
- **One-standard insistence** — refusing to consider their legitimate standard; you must behave like a judge.
- **Using criteria as ammunition** for a pre-set position rather than a joint search.
- **Yielding to pressure** because it's framed as policy/trust/threat — answer all three the same way.
- **No reciprocity test** — proposing a standard you wouldn't accept if reversed.
- **Fabricated "market rate."** If you don't have data, get it; don't invent it.
