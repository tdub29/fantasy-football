# When They Won't Play — Negotiation Jujitsu, the One-Text Procedure & Dirty Tricks

**Produce:** a go/no-go counter-play — the jujitsu reframe for their positional moves, a decision on whether to run the one-text procedure, and a dirty-tricks triage (name → category → principled counter) ending in a BATNA walk-away test.

## When to use

Trigger when the other side refuses to negotiate on the merits or plays hardball. Concrete cues:

- "They won't budge / they keep restating their position / they attack me."
- Lowball, take-it-or-leave-it, good-cop/bad-cop, escalating demands, "company policy," manufactured deadline.
- You suspect deception about facts, authority, or intentions.
- A multi-party or stalemated deal that needs a single text to converge.

For the *live verbal* parry, defer to `difficult-conversation`. This doc owns the **strategy** for an unwilling/hardball counterpart.

## The framework

### Three approaches to get them to play

1. **What you do** — keep playing principled negotiation; it's contagious ("change the game by playing a new one").
2. **What they do** — counter their positional moves with **negotiation jujitsu**.
3. **What a third party does** — the **one-text procedure**.

### Negotiation jujitsu

When they push, **don't push back.** Don't reject their position, don't defend your idea, don't counterattack. Sidestep and deflect their force onto the problem. Their attack is usually three moves; counter each:
- **Don't attack their position — look behind it.** Treat it as one option; mine the interests and principles beneath it ("What's the theory that makes $2,000 fair?"). Ask hypothetically what would happen if their position were accepted (the Nasser "what would happen to Golda Meir?" interview).
- **Don't defend your ideas — invite criticism and advice.** "What's wrong with this?" "What would you do in my shoes?" Turn criticism into raw material for improving the proposal.
- **Recast an attack on you as an attack on the problem.** "When you say the strike shows we don't care about the kids, I hear your concern *for* the kids — we share it. How do we end this fast?"

Two key tools: **ask questions, not statements** (questions generate answers and offer no target), and **use silence** — after a question or an unreasonable demand, just wait; people rush to fill it.

### The one-text procedure

When your own efforts fail, bring in a third party (or *act* as one — "mediate your own dispute" when you care more about *effecting* a deal than the exact terms). The procedure:
1. A neutral asks about **interests**, not positions (the architect: "morning sun or afternoon sun? look out or look in?").
2. Builds a list of both sides' interests; asks each to **criticize** it (easy to criticize, hard to concede).
3. Produces a draft, asks for criticism, **revises repeatedly** — no one's ego is committed to any draft.
4. When it can't be improved, **recommends** it; each side's only decision is **yes/no**, and yes can be made contingent on their yes.

It limits the number of decisions, reduces each decision's uncertainty, and stops people locking into positions. Camp David: ~23 drafts over 13 days, then both accepted. You usually **need no one's permission** to start — just prepare a draft and ask for criticism.

### Dirty tricks — negotiate the rules of the game

Tricky tactics are illegitimate because they **fail the test of reciprocity** (designed for one side only). They are one-sided proposals *about procedure* — so counter them with principled negotiation *about the process*. Three steps: **recognize the tactic, raise it explicitly, question its legitimacy.** Then apply the four principles to procedure (separate people / interests / options / objective criteria), and **as a last resort, invoke your BATNA and walk**.

Three categories with counters:
- **Deliberate deception** — *phony facts* (verify; proceed independent of trust); *ambiguous authority* (ask their authority *before* give-and-take; "nothing is agreed until everything is agreed"); *dubious intentions* (build compliance into the agreement — the contingent child-support/equity clause). Note: less-than-full disclosure ≠ deception.
- **Psychological warfare** — *stressful settings* (name it, change it); *personal attacks* (recognize → neutralize); *good-cop/bad-cop* (ask the good cop the same principled question); *threats* (warnings > threats; "I only negotiate on the merits").
- **Positional pressure tactics** — *refusal to negotiate* (find their interest in refusing; suggest channels; insist on principle); *extreme demands* (ask for principled justification until it looks ridiculous); *escalating demands* (call it out, take a break, insist on principle); *lock-in/commitment* (interrupt the communication, deflate it, joke, "I never yield to pressure, only to reason"); *hardhearted partner* (get the principle agreed, then reach the partner); *calculated delay* (create a fading opportunity / objective deadline); *take-it-or-leave-it* (ignore it first, keep talking; or give them a face-saving exit).

**"Don't be a victim."** Test your own conduct: would you use this with a friend? Would it embarrass you in the media? It's easier to defend principle than an illegitimate tactic.

## How to apply (step-by-step)

1. **Diagnose**: are they positional-but-honest (→ jujitsu), or using a *trick* (→ dirty-tricks triage)?
2. **For each positional move**, write the jujitsu reframe (look-behind / invite-criticism / recast-attack).
3. **Decide on the one-text procedure** — would a draft-and-criticize loop converge this? Who plays the third party (can it be you)?
4. **Triage any tricks**: name → category → principled counter.
5. **Set the walk line** — the BATNA point past which you stop and invoke your alternative.
6. **Sanity-check**: are you about to push back (re-entering positional bargaining) instead of deflecting onto the problem?

## Apply to Trevor's offer close

Common hardball in offer closes maps straight onto the book's tactics: **"that's the band / company policy"** (ambiguous-authority + positional pressure — ask who *can* approve an exception and on what standard); **exploding offer / "decide by Friday"** (calculated delay/deadline — create your own fading opportunity with a competing process, or ask for principled time); **"this is our best and final"** (take-it-or-leave-it — keep talking, surface a face-saving change in circumstances); **recruiter-as-good-cop, comp-committee-as-bad-cop** (ask the recruiter the same principled question). Run **jujitsu** when they restate the number: don't counter-number, ask "what's the theory behind that band?" and invite them to improve it. If pressure persists, **invoke your BATNA** (competing offer / current role) and be ready to walk — that, not willpower, is what moves a stuck close.

## Common failure modes

- **Pushing back** — rejecting/defending/counterattacking re-enters positional bargaining.
- **Filling the silence** — robbing yourself of its leverage.
- **Defending your idea** instead of asking "what's wrong with it?"
- **Attacking the person for the tactic** — name the tactic, not their integrity.
- **Tolerating or matching tricks** (Chamberlain at Munich vs. tit-for-tat escalation) — instead negotiate the rules.
- **Walking with no BATNA** — the threat to leave is empty unless the alternative is real (see `batna-best-alternative.md`).
