# Kahneman — Frames and Reality

## The framework

> "Frames and Reality." (Ch 34, lines 13293–13754)

Two presentations of identical information produce different choices. This shouldn't happen in a fully rational decision-maker — the underlying reality is identical. It happens because System 1 responds to the frame, not the substance.

### The classic Asian Disease problem

A disease will kill 600 people. Two programs proposed.

**Frame 1 (gain frame):**
- Program A: 200 people will be saved (sure thing).
- Program B: 1/3 probability that 600 will be saved, 2/3 that nobody saved.
- 72% choose A (risk-averse for gains).

**Frame 2 (loss frame):**
- Program C: 400 people will die (sure thing).
- Program D: 1/3 probability that nobody dies, 2/3 that 600 die.
- 78% choose D (risk-seeking for losses).

A=C and B=D. Same math. Different frames. Opposite preferences.

### Three sub-rules

**1. Gain frame → risk aversion; loss frame → risk seeking**
The asymmetry comes straight from prospect theory's fourfold pattern. Frame-dependent because reference-point dependent.

**2. Default options dominate**
"Opt in to organ donation" countries: ~15% participation. "Opt out" countries: ~85%. Same population; different frame produces 5-6x difference.

**3. Reference-point selection IS the frame**
- "Pay $X more for premium" vs. "Save $X by choosing basic" — same prices, different reference points.
- "5-year survival rate 90%" vs. "10% of patients die within 5 years" — same facts, different reference frames.

### The cost of framing

Most framing effects are NOT acknowledged by the framer (they didn't choose the frame strategically) NOR by the framed (they didn't notice the frame was driving them). System 1 reads the frame as if it were the reality.

## How to apply

When designing choices:

1. **Pick the frame deliberately.** Don't let the default frame emerge from convenience. Choose gain or loss frame based on what response you want (and on ethics — see below).
2. **Set defaults carefully.** Most people will accept defaults. The default is doing more than your description.
3. **Present multiple frames if possible.** Showing "90% survival / 10% mortality" alongside each other reduces frame dependence.

When evaluating choices presented to you:

1. **Reframe.** If presented as gain, restate as loss. If as loss, restate as gain. See if your preference flips.
2. **Identify the reference point.** What's being treated as "neutral"? Whose choice was that?
3. **Strip to invariant features.** What's the actual outcome distribution? Decide from that, not from the frame.

## Common failure modes

- **Ignoring frames in your own decisions.** "I'd never be swayed by wording" — yes you would, and you are.
- **Using framing manipulatively without naming it.** Choice architects who design defaults that exploit framing without disclosure are operating in ethically gray territory.
- **Confusing reframing with re-evaluating.** Reframing shows you HAVE preference reversal; it doesn't tell you which preference is "right." Both come from System 1 responding to a frame.
- **Pretending there's a "neutral" frame.** All presentations have a frame. The choice is which, not whether.

## Ethical guardrail

Framing effects can be deployed for the chooser's benefit (default opt-in to retirement savings; defaults to organ donation) OR for the framer's benefit (defaults to upsells; loss-frame to drive panic buying). The framework is morally neutral; the application isn't. Three checks:

- **Whose interest does the frame serve?** Framer or chooser?
- **Is disclosure available?** Choice architects who explain the framing they used pass a higher integrity bar.
- **Would the chooser endorse the frame if they knew it was being used?** If no, it's manipulation.
