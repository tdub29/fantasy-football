# Dan Ariely — The Cost of Social Norms

## The framework

> "The Cost of Social Norms." (Ch 4)

Two distinct sets of rules govern human behavior:

**Market norms:**
- Paid transactions.
- Fair value for services rendered.
- Contractual; obligations are explicit.
- "I paid you X; you owe me Y."

**Social norms:**
- Favors, gifts, friendship.
- No expectation of exact return.
- Long-term relational; obligations are diffuse.
- "I helped you; you'll help me sometime."

Both work. Each in its own context. The mistake: mixing them.

### The demonstration

Subjects asked to drag circles on a screen. Three conditions:
- **Paid well** ($5 per session): 159 circles dragged.
- **Paid poorly** ($0.50): 101 circles.
- **No pay, as a favor:** 168 circles.

The favor group OUTPERFORMED the well-paid group. The poorly-paid group performed worst.

The key finding: **once you mention money, you're in MARKET territory.** Even $0.50 invokes market norms. Below market rates, the work feels exploitative. The "favor" frame produces effort that markets can't pay for.

### Three sub-rules

**1. Adding money to social arrangements destroys them.**

When you pay your mother-in-law for cooking, the family dinner becomes a contract. The relationship transforms — usually badly.

Israeli daycare demonstration: introducing a fine for picking up children late INCREASED late pickups. Parents converted "social obligation" to "market transaction" — and decided $X was worth the late pickup. The fine reframed the dynamic.

**2. Removing the social frame for cost-cutting backfires.**

A company that says "we're a family here" while paying market wages can get extra effort from the social frame. When pressure rises and they invoke market norms ("you should work harder for what we pay you"), employees defect to pure market — and now want overtime, exit interviews, recruiter calls.

**3. The transition is asymmetric.**

Going from social → market is fast and irreversible. Going from market → social is slow and uncertain. Once the relationship has been monetized, you can't un-monetize.

### Where this appears

- **Hiring friends/family.** The transaction usually corrodes the relationship.
- **Volunteer organizations** that introduce stipends. Volunteer hours drop more than the stipends compensate for.
- **Startup compensation.** "Equity + mission" appeals to social norms; salary appeals to market norms. Mixing requires care.
- **Tipping.** A weird hybrid; the cultural variations show how unstable hybrids are.

## How to apply

When designing compensation or asking for help:

1. **Decide: market or social?** Pick one cleanly.
2. **If market:** pay full market rate. Explicit contract. Clear deliverables. Don't expect social effort.
3. **If social:** no money at all. Or token gestures that are clearly NOT transactional (a thank-you gift, not a payment).
4. **Beware partial introductions.** A small payment INTO a social arrangement destroys the social frame without buying market commitment.

For organizations:

1. **Identify which work is social-norm work.** Discretionary effort, helping coworkers, going above and beyond. Pay people fairly for the market-norm portion, then nurture social norms around the discretionary effort.
2. **Don't betray the social frame for cost savings.** "Family company" + layoffs is the classic destruction.

For relationships:

1. **Don't pay friends/family for what should be social.** A favor stays a favor.
2. **If you must transact with intimates: name it.** "This is a market thing. I'd like to pay you fairly. The friendship is separate."
3. **Repair takes time.** Once you've made a relationship transactional, returning to social takes years.

## Common failure modes

- **Token payments destroying social arrangements.** $20 thank-you to your nephew for fixing your laptop turns a favor into a poorly-paid contract.
- **Demanding social-norm effort while paying market rates.** "Be passionate about the mission!" at market wage — works only while the social frame is intact.
- **Using market norms to set scope.** "I paid you $X; that's all you should do." Maybe true under market norms; corrosive in any social context.
- **Romance and money.** Especially explosive territory. "I bought you dinner" → expectations leak in either direction.
- **Charity and tipping.** When you tip a service person, are you in market mode (settling a transaction) or social mode (showing appreciation)? Both, partially. The instability is real.
