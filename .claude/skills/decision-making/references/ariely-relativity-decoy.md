# Dan Ariely — The Truth About Relativity (Decoy Effect)

## The framework

> "The Truth About Relativity." (Ch 1)

People don't compute absolute value. They compute RELATIVE value — by comparing to what's nearby. Whoever frames the comparison set controls the conclusion.

### The Economist demonstration (Ch 1)

Three subscription options offered:
1. Internet-only: $59 — 16 students chose this
2. Print-only: $125 — 0 students chose this
3. Print + Internet: $125 — 84 students chose this

Then the experiment repeated WITHOUT option 2:
1. Internet-only: $59 — 68 students chose this
2. Print + Internet: $125 — 32 students chose this

The "print-only at $125" option was a DECOY. Nobody chose it directly. But its presence made the "Print + Internet for the same price" feel like an obvious bargain.

Without the decoy, students preferred the cheaper internet-only. With the decoy, students dramatically preferred the more expensive bundle.

The decoy didn't sell. It RECONFIGURED how the other options looked.

### The three properties of an effective decoy

1. **Inferior to your target.** The decoy should be dominated by your preferred option on a clear dimension.
2. **Similar to your target on other dimensions.** It compares to the target, not the cheaper option.
3. **Genuinely a real option that no one would pick.** Not a bait-and-switch — actually offered, just clearly worse than the target.

### Asymmetric dominance

> Technical name for the decoy effect: "asymmetric dominance."

The decoy is dominated by ONE option (the target you want them to choose) but NOT by the other options. The asymmetry is what creates the relative comparison.

### Where it appears

- **Pricing pages:** Three tiers, middle tier looks like the obvious choice.
- **Real estate:** Realtors show you an overpriced inferior house before the one they want to sell.
- **Menus:** The $90 entrée makes the $40 entrée feel reasonable.
- **Job offers:** A weak competing offer makes the actual target feel great.

### The defense

When evaluating options:

1. **Ask: which option is dominated by another?** That's the decoy. Mentally remove it.
2. **Re-evaluate without it.** What's the choice really between?
3. **Often the conclusion shifts.** What seemed like "obvious choice" was a relative comparison the decoy was supporting.

## How to apply

When designing pricing or choice architecture:

1. **Identify your target option** (the one you want chosen).
2. **Create a decoy** that's inferior to the target on a key dimension but priced similarly. The decoy should be a real option, just one nobody would pick.
3. **Place all three options together.** The decoy reframes the target as the bargain.

When evaluating options offered TO you:

1. **Scan for a dominated option.** Inferior on every meaningful dimension to another option.
2. **Recognize the decoy.** Strip it from comparison.
3. **Re-evaluate the remaining options on their absolute merits.** Often the decision changes.

## Common failure modes

- **Using a decoy too obviously.** Sophisticated audiences notice. The decoy must be plausible-as-an-option, just dominated.
- **No decoy when you have one good option.** Without comparison context, even a great option can be rejected. People NEED to compare.
- **Decoys for trivial decisions.** Burns design effort.
- **Decoy that compares to the wrong option.** If the decoy looks similar to the CHEAPER option, it makes cheap look like the bargain. Make sure asymmetric dominance points where you want it.

## Ethical guardrail

The decoy effect is heavily used in manipulative pricing. Three checks:

- **Are you using a decoy to highlight genuine value?** A premium option that's actually premium, made visible via a relativity-based comparison — fine.
- **Are you using a decoy to overcharge?** If the target option costs more than reasonable absolute value, you're exploiting bias, not informing.
- **Disclosure test:** Would your customer be upset to learn the middle option exists primarily as a comparison anchor? If yes, you've crossed a line.
