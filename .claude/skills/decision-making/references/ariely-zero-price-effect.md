# Dan Ariely — The Cost of Zero Cost (FREE!)

## The framework

> "The Cost of Zero Cost." (Ch 3)

FREE! is not just a low price. It's a separate cognitive category. The transition from $0.01 to $0 produces a behavioral discontinuity larger than any other price drop.

### The Hershey/Lindt demonstration (Ch 3)

Subjects offered a choice:
- Lindt truffle for $0.15 (high-quality chocolate)
- Hershey's Kiss for $0.01 (lower quality)

73% chose the Lindt. The price difference of $0.14 was outweighed by the quality difference.

Same items, both prices reduced by $0.01:
- Lindt truffle for $0.14
- Hershey's Kiss for FREE

69% chose the Hershey's Kiss. The price difference stayed at $0.14. The QUALITY difference was unchanged. But "FREE" rewired the comparison entirely.

### Why FREE has special power

1. **No downside risk.** A purchase has the risk that you'll regret it. FREE has no such risk; pure upside.
2. **Loss aversion goes to zero.** Any price triggers loss aversion. FREE removes loss aversion from the equation.
3. **Cognitive ease.** "Should I buy this for $0.14 or that for free" is processed differently than "$0.14 or $0.15."

### Where it appears

- **Free shipping thresholds.** People will add $30 of items to qualify for "free shipping" on a $5 cost. The math doesn't work; the FREE does.
- **Buy one get one FREE.** Outperforms 50% off, even though they're mathematically equivalent.
- **FREE gift with purchase.** Adds disproportionate perceived value.
- **Freemium / free trials.** The category-shift activates use that paid tiers wouldn't.

### Where it fails

- **When the FREE thing has obvious time-costs.** "FREE seminar! (3 hours of your Saturday)" — time-cost makes it not actually free.
- **When the FREE thing creates obligation.** "FREE gift" with a high-pressure sales pitch attached — people detect the trap.
- **When the FREE thing is clearly worthless.** No effect; free worthless items are still worthless.

### The reverse-application

If you're being TOO generous with free (free shipping for everyone, free upgrades, free trials too long) — you might be locking in zero-price effect that prevents reasonable monetization later. The path from FREE to paid is much harder than the path from cheap to paid.

## How to apply

When designing offers:

1. **Add FREE wherever the marginal cost is low and the conversion lift is high.** Free shipping over $X. Free trial. Free first month.
2. **Bundle FREE with a paid action.** "Buy this and get THAT free" outperforms a direct discount.
3. **Set FREE thresholds at conversion-relevant levels.** Free shipping at $50 drives basket size to $50+.

When evaluating offers:

1. **Compute the real cost of FREE.** Time, attention, downstream upsells, lock-in.
2. **Compare on absolute value, not the FREE feature.** Would I take this if it cost $0.05 instead of $0? If yes — fine. If no — the FREE is making you trade poorly.
3. **Notice when FREE is structuring your decision.** "Free shipping over $50" causing you to add $30 of unwanted items.

## Common failure modes

- **Adding FREE without margin to support it.** Free shipping costs SOMEONE. If it's you, the unit economics may not work.
- **FREE that becomes expected.** Once your category establishes FREE shipping, you can't claw it back.
- **Customer's-FREE-becomes-your-expensive.** Free trials of expensive products without conversion mechanisms = burning money.
- **Adding fake-FREE.** "Free with $X minimum purchase" is detected by sophisticated buyers as just bundling.

## Ethical guardrail

The zero-price effect is heavily used to extract behavior the customer wouldn't otherwise endorse:
- Free trials that auto-renew at painful prices.
- "FREE gift" requiring data harvesting.
- Free shipping thresholds that drive over-purchasing.

The disclosure test: would the customer mind learning what "free" actually costs them? If yes — you're exploiting the bias, not creating value.
