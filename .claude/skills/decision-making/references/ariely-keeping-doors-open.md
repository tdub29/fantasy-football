# Dan Ariely — Keeping Doors Open

## The framework

> "Keeping Doors Open." (Ch 8)

Standard rationality says: more options = better. Ariely's finding: actively keeping options OPEN has costs that we systematically underestimate.

### The demonstration

Subjects played a computer game where they could "enter" rooms to earn money. Each click of a room paid out points. The rooms started equal in expected payout.

Critical variation: rooms that weren't clicked for a few turns would START TO DISAPPEAR. Subjects could re-click them to reset the disappearance.

Rational strategy: identify the room with highest payout, click ONLY that room. Let the others disappear.

Actual behavior: subjects spent SIGNIFICANT clicks "preserving" the other rooms. Even at clear cost to total earnings. The doors disappearing was psychologically intolerable.

When the cost of preservation was increased, behavior didn't change much. Subjects kept preserving doors even at known cost.

### The three takeaways

**1. We over-value optionality.**
The presence of an option produces psychological comfort beyond its expected value. We pay (in attention, time, resources) to keep options open.

**2. We under-account for preservation cost.**
The cost of keeping an option alive is invisible at any moment. It accumulates across many moments. By the time the cost is large, we've forgotten how it started.

**3. Closing doors feels like loss, not freedom.**
Even when objectively better off closing a door, we feel as if we're losing something. Loss aversion attaches to options, not just possessions.

### Where it appears

- **Career decisions.** "I'll keep both job offers open while I think." Each day of indecision damages both.
- **Relationships.** "I'll stay in this one while keeping the new option open." Damages both.
- **Hobbies.** Six half-finished projects, none completed.
- **Education.** Multiple half-finished degrees.
- **Real estate.** Two houses, neither fully lived in.

### The opposite skill: deliberate door-closing

> The strength is in committing.

When you CLOSE doors deliberately, the remaining option benefits from:
- Full attention.
- Investment without hedging.
- Identity formation around it.
- Clear feedback (no muddying from other options).

The closer-of-doors paradoxically gets BETTER outcomes than the keeper-open.

### When doors should stay open

Not all door-keeping is irrational:
- High uncertainty + cheap to maintain = keep open.
- Information arrives soon that will resolve the decision = wait.
- Genuinely incompatible options requiring time to evaluate = take time.

The pathology is keeping doors open at clear cost when the decision could be made.

## How to apply

When you notice you've been "keeping options open" for a long time:

1. **Compute the maintenance cost.** Time spent agonizing. Half-investments. Identity-fragmentation. Opportunity cost of not committing.

2. **Compute the option's actual value.** What's the realistic upside of the option you're preserving?

3. **If maintenance cost > option value: close it.** Deliberately. Verbally. Document the decision so you don't re-open.

4. **Watch the relief.** Often closing a long-held-open door produces immediate clarity. That's diagnostic — the door was costing you more than you'd consciously accounted for.

5. **Re-direct the saved energy.** The point isn't austerity; it's putting the preserved energy into the chosen path.

For organizations:

1. **Identify "keeping options open" as a project pattern.** Half-funded initiatives, abandoned-but-not-killed projects, parallel tracks where commitment would beat hedging.
2. **Close deliberately.** A kill decision is a real decision. Frame it as such.

## Common failure modes

- **Refusing to close because "you never know."** Almost always wrong on a long-enough timescale. You usually do know, eventually.
- **Closing under emotional pressure rather than analysis.** "I'm DONE with this!" said angrily. Closing is good; closing well requires the cold-state version.
- **Closing externally but staying internally hedged.** Saying "I picked X" while keeping mental availability for Y. The benefits of closing only arrive with internal commitment.
- **Treating "options" as identity.** "I'm the kind of person who keeps options open." Self-flattery as an excuse for indecision.
