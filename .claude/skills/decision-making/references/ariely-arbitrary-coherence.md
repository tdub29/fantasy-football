# Dan Ariely — Arbitrary Coherence

## The framework

> "The Fallacy of Supply and Demand." (Ch 2)

Standard economic theory says prices reflect supply and demand. Ariely's research: prices reflect WHERE WE ANCHORED — and that anchor is often arbitrary.

### The Social Security demonstration (Ch 2)

MIT students were asked to write the last two digits of their Social Security number on a bidding sheet. Then they bid on items (wine, books, accessories). The students with high SSN digits bid 60-120% MORE than students with low SSN digits — for the SAME items.

The SSN is arbitrary. It shouldn't influence wine valuation. But once written down, it served as an anchor. Subsequent valuations clustered around it.

### "Coherent" without being right

> Once we set a first anchor, subsequent prices arrange themselves COHERENTLY relative to that anchor.

If $2 is "the price of coffee" in your head, then $4 feels expensive and $1.50 feels cheap. The relative judgments are coherent. The anchor itself ($2) might be arbitrary.

Ariely's term: **arbitrary coherence**. Coherent in structure; arbitrary in absolute level.

### How initial anchors persist

- **First price sticks.** The first time you encountered a product category at $X, that $X became your reference.
- **Decades later, you still react to other prices relative to that anchor.** Even if conditions have completely changed.
- **You confuse the reference with "real value."** It feels intrinsic. It isn't.

### Self-herding

> Ariely's variant: we follow our OWN past prices.

You paid $5 for a latte once. That became the anchor. Now $4 feels cheap and $7 feels exploitative. The original $5 might have been a high-end coffee shop, but it imprinted as "what coffee costs."

You're not just anchored to others' prices. You're anchored to YOUR OWN previous purchases.

### Why this matters more than standard anchoring

Standard anchoring (per-decision) wears off over hours. Arbitrary coherence persists for YEARS. The price you saw when you first encountered the category becomes the lifelong reference.

This explains:
- Why older people resist "the new price of things" even when inflation justifies it.
- Why a company can establish a price by being first to market.
- Why brand-new categories can be priced almost anywhere (the first price IS the anchor).

## How to apply

When setting price for a new product:

1. **First impression matters disproportionately.** Whatever you price at launch will anchor customers for years.
2. **Price high if you want premium positioning.** Hard to RAISE later; easy to DISCOUNT from.
3. **Price low if you want mass adoption.** Hard to extract premium later.
4. **For brand-new categories: invest in the anchor.** Educational content explaining "what this should cost" can establish your anchor as the category's reference.

When evaluating prices:

1. **Ask: what's my anchor for this category?** When did I form it?
2. **Is the anchor still relevant?** Conditions change; anchors don't update automatically.
3. **Examine prices against multiple references.** Cost of inputs? Competitive landscape? Value created?

## Common failure modes

- **Confusing anchor-based value with intrinsic value.** Your reference price is a function of WHEN you first encountered the category — not what the item is worth.
- **Setting a low launch price and trying to raise it.** Your customers have anchored at the low price. Resistance to increases is fierce.
- **Assuming competitors set "the right price."** They set the first anchor. Doesn't mean it's correct.
- **Ignoring that YOUR purchases anchor your future self.** That first $5 latte means future $7 lattes feel like robbery to you — and you'll resist them long after they're appropriately priced.

## Ethical guardrail

Arbitrary coherence is heavily exploited by anchor-pricing strategies. Two checks:

- **Manufactured high anchors.** Listing a "MSRP $399" next to "today $99" to make $99 feel cheap — manipulative if the MSRP was never a real sales price.
- **Reference-price manipulation.** Adjusting the comparison set to make your price look reasonable. Disclosure test: would the customer mind learning the comparison was strategic?
